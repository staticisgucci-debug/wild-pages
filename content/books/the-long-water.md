# The Long Water

## Chapter One: Smoke on the Ridge

The smoke came over the ridge like something alive.

Wren saw it from the top of the water tank, where she was absolutely not supposed to be. One minute the sky above Cutler Ridge was the ordinary washed-out blue of late August. The next minute it wasn't sky at all. It was a wall the color of an old bruise — brown and yellow and moving.

She had been counting swallows. She stopped counting swallows.

"Bramble," she said.

The dog was already up. He stood in the dry grass at the base of the tank with his ears forward and his whole body pointed at the ridge, the way he pointed at deer. Except he wasn't wagging.

Wren came down the ladder too fast. She skinned her palm on the last rung and didn't feel it until much later.

The ranch was three miles behind her. The ridge was one mile ahead. And she knew — everyone up here knew, it was the first thing you learned before you were ever allowed out past the gate — that fire runs uphill fast and downhill slow.

Which meant she was standing in exactly the wrong place for either of those facts to help her.

Then the wind changed.

She felt it as a shove against her back, warm where the afternoon had been cool, and she understood something she wished she didn't. The wind was behind her now. It was going to push the fire *off* the ridge and down the long slope, through the cheatgrass and the dead pines, straight across the road she needed.

Three miles home. In her head she watched the fire get there first.

"Okay," Wren said. Her voice came out small and strange, like it belonged to somebody younger. "Okay. Okay."

Bramble looked up at her and waited, because that is what he had always done, and because he had no idea.

There was one other way.

She turned her head slowly to the east, toward the flat grey shine at the bottom of the valley, and her stomach dropped all the way down into her boots.

The lake.

Her father called it the Long Water, because it was two miles from the near shore to the far one, and because he believed that giving a frightening thing a plain name made it smaller.

It had never once worked on Wren.

Up on the ridge behind her something popped — a whole pine going up at once, the sound arriving late, like thunder does.

Wren picked up her jacket. She whistled, low, and Bramble came.

Then she ran downhill toward the water.

## Chapter Two: What the Fire Wanted

Running downhill isn't really running. It's falling, with your feet trying to catch up.

The grass whipped her shins. Grasshoppers went off around her boots like popcorn, hundreds of them, all fleeing the same direction she was.

She did not look back. Looking back costs you a step, and she'd started counting her steps the way you count money when there isn't much.

But she could feel it. The air kept getting warmer behind her ears, and that was worse than any sound.

Halfway down, the first ash landed on her arm.

It was grey and soft and bigger than a snowflake, and it was still warm, and she brushed it off and it left a smear like charcoal. Then there was another. Then there were hundreds, drifting down through the yellow light, and the whole valley went strange and quiet and orange, like the inside of a lamp.

Bramble was running beside her with his tail low.

The shore came up fast — cracked mud, then round stones, then the water, flat and grey and going on forever.

And there, pulled up on the stones exactly where it had been every summer of her life, was Ander Pell's rowboat.

Wren nearly laughed. It was the ugliest boat in the county. Aluminum, dented all over like a can somebody had stepped on, half full of last week's rain.

One oar.

She found the second one snapped in half under the seat, which meant it had been broken for a long time, which meant nobody was coming to fix it.

"Fine," she said, and shoved.

The boat didn't move. The rainwater inside it weighed more than she did.

She climbed in and bailed with her cupped hands, then with her boot, and the whole time the light kept getting redder and the ash kept coming down and she did not look up at the slope, not once, because she had decided that she wasn't going to.

Then Bramble started barking.

Not his squirrel bark. A new one. Short and flat and awful.

Wren looked up.

The fire had come over the shoulder of the hill and it was in the cheatgrass now, and cheatgrass burns the way paper burns. It was moving down the slope in a long crooked line, and it was not the color she expected. It wasn't red. It was almost white at the bottom.

She could hear it. That was the part nobody ever tells you. It sounded like rain, like heavy rain on a metal roof, coming closer.

"Bramble. *In.*"

He wouldn't. He stood on the stones with his feet planted and his ears back, terrified of the water, and behind him the world was catching.

So Wren got out of the boat, in the last few seconds she had, and she picked up all forty pounds of him, and he did not fight her.

She set him in the bow. She pushed off with the one good oar. And the stones she had been standing on went orange behind her.

## Chapter Three: Nine Hundred Strokes

You cannot row a boat with one oar. Wren figured that out in about four seconds, spinning in a slow useless circle while the shore burned.

But you can *scull*.

Her grandfather had shown her, one dead-still evening years ago, and she had been bored the whole time and had never once thought she'd need it. You drop the oar over the back. You hold it flat. You sweep it side to side like a fish's tail, and you keep the blade angled just so, and the boat goes forward.

It went forward.

Not fast. About as fast as a person can walk, if that person is very tired.

Wren decided she would count to a hundred and then check the shore.

She counted to a hundred. She checked the shore. It looked exactly the same, except now the smoke had climbed so high above it that the sun had gone the color of a penny.

So she counted another hundred.

Ash came down on the water all around the boat, and each piece made a tiny grey ring where it landed, and there were so many rings that the whole lake looked like it was simmering. Bramble lay in the bow with his chin on the aluminum and watched her the entire time, and she talked to him, and it did not matter at all what she said.

Four hundred.

Her shoulders had stopped being shoulders. They were just two hot places where her arms attached.

Five hundred.

Six hundred, and she couldn't feel the palm she'd skinned on the ladder, and she thought about how that had happened *today*, which seemed impossible.

At seven hundred, the far shore stopped being a grey line and became trees.

She knew those trees. She knew the shape of that inlet, she knew the drowned stump at the mouth of it, she had swum out to that stump on a dare when she was nine and cried on the way back.

Wren was crying now, a little, in a plain way that didn't slow her down.

Eight hundred.

At eight hundred and something — she lost count, and she let herself lose it — the bottom of the boat touched sand, and the whole world tipped, and she went over the side into two feet of cold water and stayed there on her knees.

Bramble was out and up the bank in one motion, shaking, then rolling in the dirt, then coming back to shove his wet head under her arm like nothing had happened at all.

Behind them, across two miles of grey water, Cutler Ridge burned.

Wren sat down in the shallows and looked at it for a long time, and it was the most terrible and most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and she was allowed to think both of those things at once.

Then, up through the trees, headlights.

Two of them, bouncing hard down the fire road, moving much too fast for that road, and she knew that truck the way she knew her own hands.

Wren stood up.

She was going to have to explain the water tank.

She found, to her enormous surprise, that she did not care.
